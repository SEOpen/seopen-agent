# Writing pages that rank and get quoted

These rules are for writing a new page or rewriting an existing one. They apply to every sentence you hand back, including titles, descriptions, headings and link text.

## Facts first

- Every fact comes from `seo/facts.md`, the repo, or the user's own words. Nothing else.
- Never invent a statistic, quote, customer, review, price, benchmark, date or result. Not as a placeholder, and not as an example.
- Name a source or drop the claim. "Most teams" needs a source. If you can't name one, cut the sentence or say only what you know.
- Missing proof is a question for the user. Ask for the number, the example or the screenshot. Don't write around the gap with vague words.
- If the facts file and the repo disagree, say so to the user and use neither until they answer.

## Open with the answer

- The first two sentences answer the main search. Someone who reads only those should have what they came for.
- Detail, steps, caveats and background come after.
- Find the main search in the brief, or in the page's Search Console queries (`top_queries`, or `get_search_console` filtered to the page). Use the words people search, not the words the company uses internally.

## Headings

- Phrase headings as the questions people search, taken from the brief or the page's queries. "How long does an oak desk take to make?" beats "Lead times".
- Sentence case: capital on the first word and on names only.
- One H1, the page's main search in plain words. H2s for the main questions, H3s inside them only when a section has real parts.
- A heading says what the section answers. No clever or vague headings.

## Sections that stand alone

An AI answer often quotes one section without the rest of the page. Write each section so it still makes sense on its own.

- Name the subject. Write "The oak desk takes six weeks", not "It takes six weeks".
- Don't lean on earlier sections: no "as mentioned above", no "this" pointing back two paragraphs.
- Put the answer in the section's first sentence, then the detail.
- Keep one section to one question.

## The competitor-swap test

Read each paragraph and ask: could it sit unchanged on a competitor's site? If yes, it adds nothing. Rewrite it around something only this site knows:

- a real number from the facts file;
- a screenshot or a step from the product itself;
- a worked example with the site's own data;
- the owner's opinion, said as theirs.

If there's nothing only this site knows to say, cut the paragraph. A shorter page that's specific beats a longer one that's generic.

## Mechanism over adjectives

Say what happens and by how much. Adjectives are claims without proof. For a desk maker whose facts file says desks ship six weeks after the order and come with a 10-year guarantee:

| Instead of | Write |
|---|---|
| Fast delivery | Your desk ships six weeks after you order it |
| Built to last | Every desk comes with a 10-year guarantee |
| Fully customisable | You pick the width, the depth and the finish |

The numbers in the right column come from the facts file. If you don't know the number, ask. Don't guess one.

## AI-writing tells to remove

Before handing back, search the text for each of these and fix it.

| Tell | Fix |
|---|---|
| Em dashes and en dashes | A full stop, comma, colon or brackets. Use "to" for ranges: "5 to 10" |
| "It's not X, it's Y" and "not just X but Y" | Say what it is: "It's Y" |
| Puffery: seamless, robust, cutting-edge, game-changing, powerful, world-class, revolutionary, effortless | Delete it, or replace it with what happens and by how much |
| Inflated words | Plain ones: delve into to look at; leverage, utilize, harness to use; foster to build; elevate to raise; streamline to simplify. Cut landscape, realm, crucial and pivotal, or say what's actually at stake |
| "Serves as", "stands as", "boasts", "features" (as a verb) | "Is" or "has" |
| A trailing "-ing" clause tacked on: "...on every page, ensuring better rankings" | Cut it, or make it its own sentence with a source |
| Vague attribution: "experts say", "studies show", "many believe" | Name the source, or cut the claim |
| A filler intro about how important the topic is | Start with the answer |
| A closing summary that repeats the page ("In conclusion...") | Cut it. End on the last useful thing, or the next step |
| Rule-of-three padding: "fast, simple and reliable" when one is true | Keep the ones you can back up |
| Passive voice that hides who does what: "your order is checked" | Name who does it: "We check your order by hand" |
| Invisible Unicode: zero-width spaces, non-breaking spaces, soft hyphens | Delete them |
| Curly quotes where the repo's other pages use straight ones | Match the repo |
| Emoji in headings or lists | Delete them unless the site already uses them |

## Titles and descriptions

- Title: 30 to 60 characters. The main search near the start. The brand last, or left out if the site's title template adds it (count the template's characters).
- Description: one or two sentences, 120 to 155 characters, saying what's on the page. Use words from the top queries where they fit.
- Both are specific to the page. Two pages never share a title or description.
- Both follow the rules above: no puffery, no invented numbers.

## Internal links

- Link text close to the target page's main search. "Oak desk lead times", not "click here", "read more" or "this page".
- Put the link inside a sentence that already fits. If none fits, add one useful sentence, not a sentence written to hold a link.
- At most one new link per paragraph.
- No link blocks ("Related: A, B, C") in the body text unless the site already uses them.
- Link to the page's canonical address, in the form the site serves (trailing slash or not).

## Self-check before committing

Run through this list on the final text. Fix anything that fails, then run it again.

1. The first two sentences answer the main search.
2. Every heading is a question or phrase people search, in sentence case, and there's one H1.
3. Every section names its subject and makes sense alone.
4. Every paragraph passes the competitor-swap test.
5. Every number, price, quote, customer and claim has a source in the facts file, the repo or the user's words. Anything without one is cut or asked about.
6. A search of the text finds no em dashes, en dashes or invisible characters.
7. Nothing from the tells table is left.
8. The title is 30 to 60 characters, the description 120 to 155, and both are unique to the page.
9. Link text matches each target's main search, with no more than one new link per paragraph.
10. You could tell the user where each fact came from if they asked.
